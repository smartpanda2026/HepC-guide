Alternatives & special situations
  • Acute HCV Treat all patients with acute HCV — same pangenotypic regimens as chronic HCV (GLE/PIB × 8 weeks or SOF/VEL × 12 weeks). SVR >95%. Do not wait for spontaneous clearance.
  • Salvage Sofosbuvir / velpatasvir / voxilaprevir (SOF/VEL/VOX · Vosevi) × 12 weeks — for prior NS5A inhibitor failure. SVR12 ~96–97%.
  • Multi‑failure Multiple DAA failures (incl. SOF/VEL/VOX failure):
    • GLE/PIB + sofosbuvir + weight‑based ribavirin × 16 weeks, OR
    • SOF/VEL/VOX + ribavirin × 24 weeks.
  • Alternative Genotype 3 + compensated cirrhosis: SOF/VEL/VOX × 8–12 weeks (POLARIS‑3, SVR12 96%) where RAS testing is unavailable.
  • Alternative Grazoprevir / elbasvir (Zepatier) — downgraded to alternative (baseline RAS testing needed for genotype 1a). Withdrawn from the US market; Zepatier and Technivie are no longer available.
  • Contraindicated Decompensated cirrhosis (Child‑Pugh B/C): protease‑inhibitor–containing regimens (GLE/PIB, voxilaprevir) are contraindicated. Use SOF/VEL ± weight‑based ribavirin for 12 weeks, managed with a liver specialist / transplant center.
  • Renal CKD G4–G5 / dialysis: all first‑line DAAs can be used across CKD stages including dialysis with no dose adjustment for GLE/PIB or SOF/VEL. GLE/PIB is preferred. SOF‑based regimens were historically avoided in severe impairment but real‑world data support use with monitoring.
  • Genotyping Not required for pangenotypic regimens — reserve genotyping for specific retreatment contexts (e.g., GLE/PIB in certain prior‑failure scenarios) or when RAS testing is being considered.
🟣 HIV Coinfection — Treatment Guidance

💜 HIV/HCV Coinfection: How to Treat Safely

  • DAA efficacy is comparable to HCV monoinfection — SVR rates >95% with pangenotypic DAAs (GLE/PIB or SOF/VEL) [citation:6][citation:17].
  • All people with HIV should be on ART before or concurrently with HCV treatment; ART slows liver disease progression by preserving immune function [citation:2][citation:13].
  • Review ART for drug–drug interactions BEFORE starting DAA — this is the single most important safety step [citation:2][citation:13].
  • Avoid GLE/PIB with: ritonavir‑boosted regimens (atazanavir, darunavir, lopinavir), efavirenz, etravirine, cyclosporine.
  • Avoid SOF/VEL with: efavirenz, St. John's wort, rifampin.
  • If ART must be modified: delay DAA initiation ≥2 weeks after ART change, and delay resuming original ART ≥2 weeks after DAA completion [citation:2].
  • Monitor HIV RNA within 2–8 weeks after any ART modification to confirm virologic suppression [citation:2].
  • Screen for HBV before starting HCV treatment — HIV/HCV/HBV triple coinfection is common; HBV reactivation risk applies [citation:2][citation:13].
  • HIV coinfection is a risk factor for HCV relapse — consider prolonged RNA monitoring post‑SVR [citation:12].
Coordinate with HIV specialist — pangenotypic DAAs are included in the simplified algorithm for people living with HIV, but ART regimen selection requires specialist input.
🟡 HBV Coinfection — Treatment Guidance

💛 HBV/HCV Coinfection: How to Treat Safely

  • HBV reactivation risk is the key concern — not reduced HCV cure rates. DAA efficacy is preserved in HBV coinfection [citation:4][citation:10].
  • HBsAg‑positive patients: reactivation rate ~30% without prophylaxis (Meta‑analysis, 27 studies) [citation:9].
  • Prophylaxis with nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) — tenofovir (TDF/TAF) or entecavir — reduces reactivation risk to 19% of control (RR 0.19, 95% CI 0.06–0.58) [citation:9][citation:14].
  • Prophylaxis must be started BEFORE DAA and continued until 12 weeks after DAA completion [citation:2][citation:13].
  • Clinical trial evidence: SOF/VEL × 12 weeks + TAF pre‑treatment achieved 97.9% SVR12 in HBV/HCV coinfection, with significant improvement in liver fibrosis markers and no drug‑related adverse events [citation:4][citation:10].
  • HBsAg‑negative, anti‑HBc‑positive (isolated anti‑HBc): reactivation rate 0% in pooled data, but individual case reports exist — risk not completely excluded. Monitor HBV DNA periodically [citation:9].
  • If not using prophylaxis: monitor HBV DNA monthly; treat if >10× baseline or >1000 IU/mL [citation:2].
  • Compensated cirrhosis + HBV/HCV: same SOF/VEL + TAF approach is safe and effective, with liver stiffness and FIB‑4 improvements post‑treatment [citation:4][citation:10].
Specialist consultation recommended for HBV/HCV coinfection, especially if HBsAg‑positive, to coordinate antiviral prophylaxis and monitoring.

⚠️ Potential Side Effects of HCV Antivirals

🩺 Adverse reaction profile by regimen

Glecaprevir / Pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB)
Common (≥5%): headache (13%), fatigue (11%), nausea (8%).
Rare but serious: acute kidney injury, acute interstitial nephritis, possible ANCA‑associated vasculitis.
Discontinuation rate: 0.1% in trials.
Sofosbuvir / Velpatasvir (SOF/VEL)
Common: headache, fatigue, nausea (~10% any AE; none grade ≥3 in a Japanese cohort).
Rare: bilateral neuroretinitis reported.
Generally well tolerated — discontinuation due to AEs ~1%.
Sofosbuvir / Velpatasvir / Voxilaprevir (SOF/VEL/VOX)
Common: diarrhea, bronchitis (alone); fatigue, anemia, gastroenteritis, nausea (with ribavirin).
Rare: grade 3 hyperglycemia — incidence 0.015%; monitor glucose in diabetics, especially genotype 3.
Discontinuation: ≤1%.
Grazoprevir / Elbasvir (EBR/GZR)
Common: fatigue (13–23%), headache (12–20%), nausea (9–15%), diarrhea (10%).
No discontinuations due to AEs in key trials.
Note: withdrawn from US market.
Ribavirin (RBV) — adjunct in decompensated cirrhosis
Common: hemolytic anemia (61% in one series), hypocalcemia (58%), hypomagnesemia (46%).
Requires monitoring: CBC, electrolytes; avoid in pregnancy (teratogenic).
Class‑wide notes (all DAAs)
HBV reactivation: risk in HBsAg‑positive patients — screen and monitor/prophylax.
Hepatotoxicity: rare, mainly in cirrhotics; discontinue for ALT ≥10× baseline.
DDIs: most common cause of preventable AEs.
Most AEs are mild and self‑limited. Serious events are rare. Always reconcile medications and monitor high‑risk patients (cirrhosis, renal impairment, diabetes).
